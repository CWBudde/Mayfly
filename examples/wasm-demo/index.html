Two populations, one optimum

The mayfly algorithm keeps males and females apart on purpose. Males chase their own best position and the swarm's; females are drawn to a better male, or fly at random when there isn't one. Mating mixes the two. That asymmetry is the whole algorithm, and it is invisible in a printed convergence curve.

So watch it instead. Every position below is computed by github.com/cwbudde/mayfly compiled to WebAssembly and running in this tab. JavaScript draws the pixels and nothing else.

Swarm

Same seed reproduces the run exactly — same trail, same final cost. Reproducibility is a library guarantee, not a demo trick.

Convergence

Best cost after each iteration, log scale. The interesting part is the tail: that is where the variants stop agreeing.

Swarm spread

Mean distance from each mayfly to its population's centre. High is exploration, near-zero is exploitation — and a curve that hits zero early is a swarm that gave up.

Cost distribution

Every mayfly at the current iteration, sorted best to worst. Watch the spread collapse as selection pressure takes hold.

What you are looking at

Above two dimensions the view is a projection: two axes are plotted and the rest of each position is pinned at the known optimum for the heatmap. The optimizer still searches every dimension — the swarm can look motionless while moving fast somewhere you cannot see.

A run is computed in full before it is drawn, then replayed on the scrubber's clock. At these sizes a complete run takes a few milliseconds, so there is nothing to wait for and nothing to cancel.
